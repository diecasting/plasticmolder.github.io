## Medical Plastic Injection Molding at Production Scale

Medical programs carry a different weight than commercial ones because a molding defect is not a returns label — it is a patient risk. For device and component sourcing teams, the question is whether a molder treats cleanliness, documentation, and consistency as the product rather than as overhead. A supplier who understands that is the difference between a vendor and a regulated manufacturing partner.

A medical molding program is a closed loop of biocompatible material control, clean processing, validated tooling, and recorded verification. When those elements are managed together, every shipment is reproducible and auditable. When they are treated as separate steps, contamination and variation enter silently and surface only in the field.

## What the Capability Numbers Mean for Your Program

Specifications matter differently in medical molding. A 50-ton to 400-ton press range covers the small, precise parts that dominate disposables and device internals without oversizing the process. Tolerances of ±0.01 mm on critical features describe the envelope in which a fluid-path seal or a drug-delivery fit is reliable rather than marginal.

Mold life rated at 500,000 to 1,000,000 shots is a maintenance and validation plan, not a brochure claim. Tools are serviced by shot count and re-validated, so wear never surprises a regulated shipment. Tooling lead time of 20 to 40 days reflects the added discipline of validated steel and documented tryout, and an annual capacity above 30,000,000 parts signals a floor built for sustained medical volume.

## How a Medical Program Runs

Every medical program at the Dongguan facility follows a disciplined, documented path with cleanroom controls and an owner and a record at each step:

1. **Design for manufacturability (DFM).** Wall thickness, draft, gating, and tolerance stack-up are reviewed against device function and molding reliability before steel is cut.
2. **Validated tool fabrication.** The mold is machined from selected steel, fitted, and validated on a tryout press with documented results retained for the device file.
3. **Biocompatible material control.** Resin is dried, lot-verified to the specified grade, and handled to prevent contamination from receiving through molding.
4. **Cleanroom molding.** Molten thermoplastic is injected and packed under controlled, monitored conditions appropriate to the component class.
5. **Controlled handling.** The part is removed and routed with clean handling to inspection or downstream operations, minimizing human and particulate variation.
6. **Verification and traceability.** Critical dimensions are checked with CMM and gauges; results feed lot records, UDI marking, and audit-ready documentation.

The point of this structure is auditability. It is what lets a notified body or your QA team confirm a shipment matches the validated sample, and what lets a complaint investigation find root cause quickly.

## Choosing the Right Resin

Material choice in medical molding drives biocompatibility, sterilization survival, and regulatory exposure more than any other decision. The table below maps common medical thermoplastics to the properties that matter in device sourcing.

| Resin | Key properties | Typical medical applications |
|-------|----------------|----------------------|
| Medical-grade PC | Clarity, impact, heat resistance | Housings, reservoirs |
| PP (Polypropylene) | Chemical resistance, low cost | Disposables, fluid paths |
| PEEK | High strength, sterilization survival | Implant-adjacent, surgical |
| PE (Polyethylene) | Low extractables, lubricity | Tubing connectors, liners |
| TPU | Soft-touch, sealing | Catheters, grips |
| COP / COC | Clarity, low leachables | Diagnostic, drug-delivery |

Selection is matched to function, sterilization method, and regulatory requirements. For USP Class VI, ISO 10993, or specific extractable limits, the grade is qualified and the documentation your regulatory team needs is maintained from the first lot.

## Cost and Lead-Time Drivers

Buyers often fixate on piece price, but the total cost of a medical molding program is set earlier and elsewhere:

- **Tool steel and cavitation** — more cavities lower piece price but raise validated tool cost; the right balance depends on volume.
- **Resin grade** — medical and implantable grades cost more and mold differently.
- **Tolerance and finish** — tighter specs and critical surfaces add process and validation cost.
- **Secondary operations** — marking, welding, and assembly add handling but can lower your landed cost versus multiple suppliers.
- **Volume and cadence** — steady runs amortize setup and stabilize pricing.

Lead time is dominated by validated tooling, not molding. A well-run DFM and a tool shop in the same building compress the critical path from design release to first article.

## Common Defects and How They Are Prevented

Warpage, sink marks, short shots, flash, and particulate contamination are symptoms of process ignorance or weak controls, not bad luck. They are prevented by understanding how melt temperature, injection speed, packing pressure, and cooling time interact for each resin family — and by validating those settings on a tryout press before a production commit. Cavity-pressure monitoring confirms every shot fills completely, clean handling limits contamination, and SPC charts flag drift before it becomes scrap.
