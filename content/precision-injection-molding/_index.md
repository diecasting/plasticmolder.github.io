## Precision Injection Molding at Production Scale

Precision molding is where the drawing tolerance is the product. For engineering and sourcing teams, the question is whether a molder can hold a critical dimension on the millionth part as well as the first article — and prove it with measurement, not assurance. That is the gap between a shop that can make a good part once and a partner who can make a million identical ones.

A precision molding program is a closed loop of tool steel quality, press stability, resin consistency, and verified measurement. When those elements are managed together, the part is in tolerance shipment after shipment. When any one drifts — steel wear, barrel variation, moisture in resin — the dimension walks and scrap climbs before anyone notices.

## What the Capability Numbers Mean for Your Program

A 50-ton to 400-ton press range covers the small, high-cavity tools that dominate connectors and micro-features without oversizing the process. ## How a Precision Program Runs

Every precision program at the Dongguan facility follows a disciplined, measured path, and each step has an owner and a record:

1. **Design for manufacturability (DFM).** Wall thickness, draft, gating, and tolerance stack-up are reviewed so the critical dimension is achievable and stable.
2. **Stable tool fabrication.** The mold is machined from selected steel with tight cavity tolerances and validated on a tryout press with CMM data.
3. **Resin conditioning.** Resin is dried and conditioned to specification so moisture and lot variation do not move the dimension.
4. **Molding.** Molten thermoplastic is injected, packed under controlled pressure, and cooled to a stable, repeatable shape.
5. **Measured ejection and handling.** The part is removed by robot and routed to inspection with minimal handling variation.
6. **Verification.** Critical dimensions are checked with CMM and optical gauges; results feed SPC charts and lot records.

The point of this structure is proven conformance. It is what lets your incoming inspection confirm rather than discover.

## Choosing the Right Resin

Resin choice drives dimensional stability and warp more than any other decision in precision molding. The table below maps common thermoplastics to precision properties.

| Resin | Key properties | Typical precision applications |
|-------|----------------|----------------------|
| POM | Low warp, dimensional stability | Gears, bushings |
| LCP | Flow, stability, low warp | Connectors, thin walls |
| PEEK | High stability, heat resistance | Medical, aerospace-grade |
| PBT | Dimensional stability, insulation | Terminals, connectors |
| PC | Clarity, stability | Optical, lenses |
| PA6 / PA66 | Strength, wear resistance | Structural precision parts |

Selection is matched to function, environment, and regulatory requirements. For low-warp or tight-tolerance grades, the specific lot is qualified and documented from the first run.

Precision molding appears wherever a dimension is the function. Each application carries its own failure mode, and process controls are tuned to the requirement rather than applied generically:

- **Connectors and terminals** — dimensional precision and flatness are non-negotiable.
- **Optical and lensed components** — surface and form drive performance.
- **Micro-feature gears and bushings** — concentricity and finish drive wear life.
- **Medical micro components** — consistency protects function and patient safety.
- **Precision housings** — fit and sealing dominate.

For electronics and medical programs, precision components are produced from stable, qualified resins under controlled process discipline, where a single out-of-tolerance dimension can disable an assembly.

## Cost and Lead-Time Drivers

Buyers often fixate on piece price, but the total cost of a precision program is set earlier and elsewhere:

- **Tool steel and cavitation** — stable steel and more cavities raise tool cost but lower piece price at volume.
- **Resin grade** — low-warp and specialty grades cost more and mold differently.
- **Tolerance and finish** — mirror polish and ±0.01 mm add process and measurement cost.
- **Secondary operations** — inspection, marking, and assembly add handling but can lower landed cost versus multiple suppliers.
- **Volume and cadence** — steady runs amortize setup and stabilize pricing.

Lead time is dominated by validated tooling, not molding. A well-run DFM and a tool shop in the same building compress the critical path from design release to first article.

## Common Defects and How They Are Prevented

Warp, sink, short shots, flash, and dimensional drift are symptoms of process ignorance, not bad luck. They are prevented by understanding how melt temperature, injection speed, packing pressure, and cooling time interact for each resin family — and by validating those settings on a tryout press before a production commit. Cavity-pressure monitoring and CMM verification confirm every shot is in tolerance, and SPC charts flag drift before it becomes scrap.
